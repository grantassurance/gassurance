When Documents Are Not Enough: Scientific Testing in Timber Due Diligence

When the UK regulator tested 13 plywood products against their declared species, nine did not match. A test answers a question that documents cannot, provided the importer asks the right one of the right method.

Adam Grant Grant Assurance
← Back to Insights

Key points

  • Wood anatomy identifies the timber, usually to genus. DNA can identify species and sometimes origin. Stable isotopes indicate origin and not species
  • A test is only as good as the reference data behind it, and that data is thin for most traded species
  • Neither UKTR nor EUDR requires testing. Both require risk to be reduced to negligible, and a test is one way to show it
  • A matching result confirms species or origin. It does not confirm legal harvest

In 2015 the National Measurement Office, then the UK enforcement body for timber, examined 16 companies importing plywood from China. Fourteen had inadequate due diligence systems. It also bought 13 of their products and tested them under the microscope, and nine did not match the species the companies had declared.

The regulator still tests. In September 2024 the Department for Business and Trade, parent of the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS), published a notice seeking suppliers for anatomic and isotopic testing of timber products against their species declarations, with an estimated value of £200,000. An importer who has never tested a product is relying on documents that the enforcement body does not take on trust.

What each method can show

MethodWhat it answersMain limits
Wood anatomyWhat the timber is, usually to genus and sometimes to speciesSays nothing about origin. Close relatives look alike
DNA analysisSpecies, with precision. Origin, where population data existsReference data covers few species. DNA degrades in heated, glued or treated products
Stable isotope analysisWhere the tree grew, from the chemical signature of climate and soilDoes not identify species. Works at regional scale, not plot scale
Mass spectrometry (DART-TOFMS)Species, as a fast screen with little sample preparationReference coverage still limited

Wood anatomy is the quickest and cheapest, and it is what the regulator used on the plywood. It will show that a face veneer sold as birch is poplar. It will not show whether the birch grew in Latvia or Russia.

That second question is one for isotopes or DNA. Origin testing compares the sample with reference material collected from known locations, and the result is a statement of consistency with a region.

What a test cannot do

Reference data is the constraint. Building it means collecting and profiling samples across a species' whole range, which takes years, and it has been done thoroughly for a small number of high-value or high-risk species. Ask the laboratory what reference material it holds for the species and region in question before commissioning anything.

Processing is the second limit. Plywood, MDF and paper can defeat DNA extraction, which is why anatomy remains the working method for panel products.

A result also needs reading correctly. A match shows the timber is the declared species, or is consistent with the declared region. It does not show the harvest was legal. A mismatch shows the documents are wrong about one thing and puts every other statement from that supplier in doubt.

"A matching result confirms species or origin. It does not confirm legal harvest."

Where testing has decided cases

In 2015 a US mill owner, Harold Kupers, pleaded guilty under the Lacey Act to trafficking bigleaf maple taken from Gifford Pinchot National Forest. Double Helix and the University of Adelaide matched DNA from wood at his mill to the stumps in the forest. He was the first mill owner convicted under the Act for trafficking illegally harvested wood across state lines.

Documents alone failed in a more recent case. In April 2026 Boise Cascade pleaded guilty to a Lacey Act felony and was fined $6.38 million over hardwood plywood made in China and shipped through Malaysia, where it was moved into new containers before export to the United States. The country of origin on the paperwork was false.

When a UK importer should test

The UK Timber Regulation requires an operator to reduce the risk of illegal timber to negligible, and lists third-party verification among the measures it can use. Testing is worth its cost where a specific claim carries the risk:

  • Species substitution. Composite products from long supply chains, above all plywood, where face and core species are declared separately and rarely checked.
  • Origin laundering. Birch and oak products routed through third countries, where Russian or Belarusian origin would breach UK sanctions as well as UKTR.
  • High-value species with protected look-alikes, including those listed under CITES.
  • A supplier whose documents never vary from one shipment to the next.

The EU Deforestation Regulation works the same way. It does not mandate testing, and the operator's risk assessment under Article 10 is stronger with a physical check behind it. Isotope results can support a declared region. They cannot confirm a plot, so they complement geolocation and do not replace it. Our article on supply chain traceability sets out the record a test should sit within.

Claims made to customers add a second reason. "European oak" on a product page is a statement of origin, and the Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive has required substantiation of sourcing claims made to EU consumers since 27 September 2026. In the UK the Competition and Markets Authority can fine a business up to 10% of global turnover for misleading claims.

How to commission a test

Decide the question first: species, origin or both. Take the sample from the consignment itself, record who took it and when, and keep it sealed until it reaches the laboratory. A sample the supplier posts to you proves little. Send the species declaration with it, so that the laboratory tests against a stated claim.

Test a few products each year, chosen by risk, and file each result with the consignment record it relates to. One clean result does not clear a supplier for good.
