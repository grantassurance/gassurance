UK Deforestation Rules: What Defra's June 2026 Announcement Means for Timber Importers

Defra plans due diligence rules for seven commodities in Great Britain, applying to businesses with turnover above £1 million and built around the data EUDR requires. Legislation is expected in 2027.

Adam Grant Grant Assurance
← Back to Insights

Key points

  • Defra confirmed on 23 June 2026 that it will legislate under the Environment Act 2021 and strengthen the UK Timber Regulation
  • The proposed threshold is £1 million annual turnover. The commodities are wood, cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber and soy
  • The GB standard is legal production in the country of origin. Defra intends to move to a deforestation-free standard later and has given no date
  • EUDR applies in Northern Ireland from 30 December 2026

On 23 June 2026 Defra set out how Great Britain will regulate forest risk commodities. The policy paper, updated on 2 September, replaces the narrow scheme proposed in December 2023 with one far closer to the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR). Timber importers who have worked under the UK Timber Regulation (UKTR) since 2013 should read it as notice that the evidence they hold will have to change.

What Defra announced

The government intends to make regulations under the Environment Act 2021 and to bring forward separate legislation strengthening UKTR. Businesses in Great Britain with annual turnover above £1 million that use forest risk commodities or wood products would have to carry out due diligence showing that those goods were produced in compliance with local law in the country of production.

The rules would cover wood, cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber and soy, together with certain derived products. Defra names chocolate and furniture as examples. A business in scope would have three duties: to establish a due diligence system, to report on its activity, and to hold proof of compliance that includes geolocation data on the origin of the specific products.

Defra expects the legislation in 2027 and will consult businesses, civil society and international partners on the substance first. The list of derived products, the form of reporting and the enforcement model are all still open.

How far this moves from the 2023 proposal

The scheme announced on 9 December 2023 covered four commodities: non-dairy cattle products, cocoa, palm oil and soy. It applied only to organisations with global turnover above £50 million that used more than 500 tonnes of a commodity a year. Timber sat outside it and was left to UKTR.

The new threshold is one fiftieth of the old one. It brings in mid-sized importers, merchants and manufacturers that the 2023 scheme would never have reached, and it places wood inside the same framework as the agricultural commodities.

Where the GB rules differ from EUDR

The standard is the main difference. The GB rules ask whether the commodity was produced legally. EUDR asks that and also whether the land was deforested after 31 December 2020. Timber from land lawfully cleared in 2022 would pass the GB test and fail the EU one. Defra says it intends to move to a deforestation-free standard in due course.

"Timber from land lawfully cleared in 2022 would pass the GB test and fail the EU one."

The data is where the two converge. Defra's stated aim is that the information a GB business must hold will be broadly the same as that needed for a due diligence statement when exporting to the EU or moving goods to Northern Ireland. Geolocation is the element UKTR has never required.

The reporting mechanism is undecided. EUDR requires the operator to submit a due diligence statement through the EU Information System before placing each product on the market. Defra refers to reporting on activity and has not said whether that means a statement per consignment or a periodic return.

What happens to UKTR

UKTR stays in force and will be strengthened, not repealed. Today it requires the operator who first places timber on the GB market to run a due diligence system of information gathering, risk assessment and risk mitigation. Traders must keep supplier and customer records for five years. It has no turnover threshold, no geolocation requirement and no statement to a regulator.

The policy paper does not say whether the £1 million threshold will apply to timber, or whether operators below it keep their existing UKTR duties. Our reading is that the existing duties will continue for every operator regardless of size, with the geolocation and reporting duties added above the threshold. The consultation should settle the point.

Northern Ireland

EUDR applies in Northern Ireland directly. Large and medium operators must comply from 30 December 2026. Micro and small operators must comply from the same date for wood products now covered by the EU Timber Regulation, and from 30 June 2027 for other products. The Office for Product Safety and Standards will be the competent authority for wood and rubber, and DAERA for the other five commodities.

The UK is classified as low risk under Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093, so goods produced in Great Britain qualify for simplified due diligence. Defra has promised guidance on movements from Great Britain to Northern Ireland and had not published it when the paper was last updated. A GB merchant shipping imported timber to a Northern Ireland customer is supplying into an EUDR market and should expect requests for geolocation and due diligence statement reference numbers from December.

What timber importers should do now

Collect to the EUDR Article 9 standard, which our guide to EUDR for UK businesses sets out. Add the geolocation of harvest plots to the information requested from suppliers, starting with the highest-volume product lines and with origins classified as standard or high risk. Record it against the consignment, because both sets of rules tie evidence to specific products.

Check turnover against the £1 million line. Defra has not said whether it will be measured at entity or group level, and for many family-owned merchants the answer decides whether they are in scope.

Respond to the consultation when it opens. The basis of the threshold, the treatment of timber under a strengthened UKTR and the reporting format will determine most of the cost.

EU digital product passports follow for furniture, which the Commission's working plan schedules for 2028. A business that already holds origin data at consignment level will be better placed to supply one.
