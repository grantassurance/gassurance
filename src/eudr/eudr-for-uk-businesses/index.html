EUDR for UK Businesses: Who Is Caught and What It Requires

The EU Deforestation Regulation applies from 30 December 2026. A UK company is caught through its EU customer, through its own EU entity, or through Northern Ireland, and each route carries a different duty.

Adam Grant Grant Assurance
← Back to Insights

Key points

  • Large and medium operators and traders must comply from 30 December 2026. Micro and small operators follow on 30 June 2027, except for timber already covered by the EU Timber Regulation, which stays at 30 December 2026
  • The first operator placing a product on the EU market submits the due diligence statement. Downstream operators and traders keep reference numbers and records for five years
  • Country of production is where the tree grew or the crop was harvested. UK manufacture does not make imported timber low risk
  • The maximum fine must be at least 4% of the operator's annual EU turnover

Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 applies from 30 December 2026. Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 moved the date by a year in December 2025 and the Commission's simplification review of 4 May 2026 left it where it was. UK companies selling timber, furniture, paper, rubber, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, soy or cattle products into the EU need to know which of three positions they occupy.

Three ways a UK company is caught

The GB exporter with an EU customer is the most common case. The EU importer places the goods on the market and is the operator. It must submit a due diligence statement before customs releases the consignment, and it cannot do so without the exporter's data. The legal duty sits with the customer. The commercial consequence sits with the supplier, who loses the order if the data does not arrive.

The second case is the UK company that acts as importer of record in the EU, typically under delivered duty paid terms, or that trades through an EU subsidiary. That company or subsidiary is the operator and carries the full obligation. Where a business established outside the EU places goods on the market, Article 7 also treats the first EU-established business that makes them available as an operator. Customers of UK firms selling on those terms will ask for evidence that due diligence has been done.

Northern Ireland is the third. EUDR applies there directly, and a movement from Great Britain to a Northern Ireland buyer is a supply into an EUDR market.

Products in scope

Annex I lists the products by customs code under seven commodities: cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soya and wood. For timber the list runs from logs and sawnwood through plywood, particleboard, joinery, pulp, paper and wooden furniture. Printed products left scope in December 2025.

Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/2102, in force since 18 September 2026, amended Annex I. It removed cattle hides and leather and several rubber articles, and added soluble coffee and certain palm derivatives from 30 December 2027. Wood, paper and furniture entries are unchanged. Check the product code against the current Annex I before assuming a product is in or out. Our Timber Import Scope Checker does this for wood products.

Operator, downstream operator or trader

Since the December 2025 amendment, only the first operator placing a product on the EU market carries out due diligence and submits a statement. That operator collects the Article 9 information, assesses risk under Article 10 and, where the risk is more than negligible, mitigates it under Article 11 before submitting.

A downstream operator makes a new product from goods already covered by a statement. A German furniture maker using oak boards that a Polish importer has declared is one. Downstream operators and traders do not repeat due diligence. They keep the names and addresses of suppliers and customers and, where the supplier is an operator, the statement reference number, for five years. Those that are not SMEs must register in the EU Information System and must verify that due diligence was done if they receive a substantiated concern.

What a UK supplier has to provide

Article 9 sets the list. The operator needs the product description and quantity, the country of production, the geolocation of every plot where the commodity was produced, the date or period of production, the supplier's details, and evidence that the product is deforestation-free and legally produced. Legal production covers land use rights, environmental and forest law, third-party rights, labour rights, tax and anti-corruption rules in the producing country.

Geolocation is the requirement UK exporters are least prepared for. A plot of four hectares or less needs one point of latitude and longitude. For commodities other than cattle, a plot above four hectares needs a polygon describing its perimeter. The operator uploads the file in GeoJSON format, and the data must relate to the consignment supplied.

FSC and PEFC certificates support the risk assessment. They do not replace it, and neither scheme's chain of custody passes plot coordinates down the chain by default.

Where UK origin helps and where it does not

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093 classifies the UK as low risk. Operators sourcing products of UK origin apply simplified due diligence: they collect the Article 9 information, including geolocation, and omit the risk assessment and mitigation steps unless they learn of a specific concern. Sitka spruce sawn in Scotland from Scottish forests qualifies.

"A table made in Yorkshire from Malaysian meranti is a Malaysian product for EUDR purposes."

A table made in Yorkshire from Malaysian meranti does not. The country of production is Malaysia, which is standard risk, and the EU operator needs plot data from the Malaysian concession and a full risk assessment. The same rule catches re-exports. French oak imported into the UK, machined and sold back to an EU buyer needs a new statement supported by the French plot data, so the UK manufacturer must obtain that data when it buys the oak.

Russia, Belarus, Myanmar and North Korea are classified high risk. Authorities must check 9% of operators sourcing from high-risk countries each year, against 3% for standard risk and 1% for low risk.

Penalties

Article 25 leaves penalties to member states and sets a floor. The maximum fine must be at least 4% of the operator's total annual turnover in the EU. Authorities can confiscate the goods and the revenue from them, exclude the operator from public procurement and public funding for up to 12 months, and prohibit it from placing products on the market. They publish final judgments against companies.

A UK exporter with no EU entity is outside the reach of those fines. It is exposed through its contracts. EU buyers are already writing EUDR warranties and indemnities into supply agreements, and a seized consignment becomes a claim against the supplier.

What comes after EUDR

Three further measures draw on the same information. Great Britain will have its own regime: Defra confirmed in June 2026 that it will require due diligence, including geolocation, from businesses with turnover above £1 million, with legislation expected in 2027. Our article on the UK deforestation rules sets out the detail.

The Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive has applied in the EU since 27 September 2026. It prohibits generic environmental claims that the trader cannot substantiate and sustainability labels that lack third-party certification. A "sustainably sourced" label on timber products sold to EU consumers now needs the evidence behind it.

The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation will require digital product passports for priority product groups, with furniture on the Commission's working plan for 2028. The product rules will decide what a furniture passport must contain.

A UK business that collects plot-level origin data once, in a form tied to consignments, meets the EUDR requests it is receiving now and starts the other three with the hardest evidence already in hand.
