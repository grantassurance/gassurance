Supply Chain Traceability: What UK Importers Need to Hold

A list of suppliers is a map. Traceability is the record that ties one consignment to the forest it came from, and four sets of rules now ask UK importers for the same record.

Adam Grant Grant Assurance
← Back to Insights

Key points

  • Mapping names the companies in a supply chain. Traceability follows a specific consignment through them to its origin
  • UKTR already requires species, country of harvest and, where risk varies within a country, the region and concession
  • EUDR and the planned GB rules add plot geolocation and tie the evidence to each consignment
  • Certification claims, mixed products and re-used documents are where the link to origin most often breaks

Most UK timber importers can name their suppliers, and many can name the mills behind them. Fewer can show which forest supplied the boards in a given container. Regulators ask the second question, and from 30 December 2026 EU customers will ask it of every consignment.

Mapping, traceability and transparency

The three terms are used as if they were one. Mapping identifies who is in the chain: the exporter, the mill, the logging contractor, the concession holder. It is usually built by questionnaire and it describes relationships.

Traceability follows a specific volume of material. It shows that the meranti in an invoice dated 3 March left a named mill, which bought logs under named permits from a named concession, harvested in a stated period. A map without that thread shows where timber could have come from.

Transparency is a decision about disclosure. A business chooses how much of its map and its traceability records to show customers or the public.

What each regulation asks for

The UK Timber Regulation (UKTR) has required traceability information since 2013. The operator must hold a description of the product, the species, the country of harvest, the quantity, the supplier's details and documents indicating legal harvest. Where the risk of illegal harvest varies within a country, it must also hold the sub-national region and the concession. Traders keep records of who supplied them and whom they supplied for five years.

The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) asks for the same items and adds two. The first is the geolocation of every plot of land where the commodity was produced, as a point for plots of four hectares or less and a polygon above that. The second is the date or period of production, which is what shows the plot was not deforested after 31 December 2020. Both must relate to the consignment supplied. Our guide to EUDR for UK businesses sets out the full list.

Great Britain's planned rules will follow. Defra's June 2026 policy paper says businesses with turnover above £1 million will hold proof of compliance that includes geolocation data on the origin of the specific products, broadly matching what an EUDR due diligence statement needs. Our article on the UK deforestation rules covers the proposal.

Two EU product measures draw on the same record. Since 27 September 2026 the Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive has required evidence behind environmental claims made to consumers, including sourcing claims. Digital product passports under the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation are scheduled to reach furniture from 2028.

The record to hold

One file per consignment answers all of them. It should contain:

  • Product description, customs code and quantity
  • Species by scientific name
  • Country of harvest, and the region and concession or forest management unit
  • Geolocation of the harvest plots
  • Harvest date or period
  • Harvest permit, transport and export documents that link the plots to the shipment
  • Supplier and customer details
  • The risk assessment and any mitigation carried out

The test of the file is the reference that joins it together. A purchase order, invoice or batch number should appear on the commercial documents and on the origin evidence. Without a common reference the file is a collection of true documents about different timber.

Where records break

Documents record what a supplier says happened. A harvest permit shows that a volume was authorised from a concession, and it does not show that the logs at the mill came from there. Checking volumes helps: a mill that exports more of a species than its declared sources could have supplied has another source.

Certification claims break the link in a different way. An FSC Mix or PEFC percentage claim is an accounting statement about a certified organisation's inputs over time. It does not identify the forest that supplied a particular product, and neither scheme passes plot coordinates down the chain as standard. A certificate supports a risk assessment. It does not replace origin data.

Composite products are harder still. Plywood, MDF, particleboard and paper draw on many sources at once. For these the operator needs every plot that could have contributed to the consignment, and the mill needs a system that can report it.

Re-used evidence is the easiest failure to spot. A supplier who sends the same concession map with every shipment, whatever the species or season, is supplying a template.

Where to start

Take the product line with the largest sales into the EU or Northern Ireland and trace one recent consignment to the forest. The exercise shows what the supplier can produce and which documents do not join up. Allow about three months for a first trace.

Then write the requirement into purchase terms. Suppliers provide geolocation and harvest dates reliably when the order depends on it.
