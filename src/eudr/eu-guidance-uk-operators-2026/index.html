EUDR Direction of Travel — What the EU's Latest Guidance Means for UK Operators

The European Commission has published its long-awaited simplification review of the EU Deforestation Regulation. The package aims to cut compliance costs by around 75%, but the core obligations remain intact. The implications apply to all operators placing relevant products on the EU market — this article looks at them through a UK lens.

Key points

  • The Commission's simplification report does not reopen the EUDR text — changes will come through guidance, FAQs, IT system design and targeted secondary legislation
  • The package is forecast to cut annual compliance costs by around 75%, mainly through streamlined due diligence and risk-based supervision
  • SMEs and downstream operators see the biggest reductions; first placers on the EU market retain full obligations
  • Application dates are unchanged: 30 December 2026 for large and medium operators, 30 June 2027 for micro and small
  • UK operators sourcing into the EU should treat the simplification as clarification, not relief — and continue building their due diligence systems

Updated October 2026 to reflect the Annex I delegated regulation of September 2026 and Defra's June 2026 announcement on Great Britain's deforestation rules.

On 4 May 2026 the European Commission published the simplification review of the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) it was required to deliver under Regulation 2025/2650. Alongside the report to the European Parliament and Council, the Commission released an updated package of guidance, a refreshed FAQ set, and signalled further changes to the EUDR Information System and Annex I via delegated and implementing acts. Together these form the "April 2026 simplification package" — published a few days late, but largely as expected.

For UK operators preparing for EUDR application from 30 December 2026, the publication is significant — but it is more a clarification of direction than a substantive change in obligation. The fundamentals are intact, the deadlines are unchanged, and most of the cost relief is concentrated at the SME and downstream end of the supply chain.

What the Commission has actually done

The most important thing to understand is what the Commission has not done. It has not reopened the EUDR's basic text. Commissioner Roswall signalled this position consistently from January 2026 onwards, and the published package confirms it. Instead, the Commission has chosen to use the tools already available to it: technical guidance, a consolidated FAQ, improvements to the EU information system, and targeted delegated and implementing acts on product scope and IT functionality.

This matters because it means the legal architecture of the EUDR — the obligation on operators to submit a due diligence statement, the requirement for plot-level geolocation, the no-deforestation cut-off date of 31 December 2020, and the legality requirement against the laws of the country of production — is unchanged. What changes is how that architecture is implemented in practice.

The 75% cost reduction — where it actually comes from

The headline figure attached to the package is an estimated 75% reduction in annual compliance costs compared with the original EUDR design. That number deserves scrutiny. It is a Commission projection based on the cumulative effect of the December 2025 amendments and the April 2026 measures combined, and it is unevenly distributed across the supply chain.

The largest savings sit with two groups: downstream operators and traders, who can rely on an upstream DDS identifier rather than repeat the full due diligence process; and micro and small primary operators, who can submit a one-time simplified declaration in place of a full DDS, and substitute postal addresses for plot-level geolocation coordinates.

"The 75% figure is a system-wide average. For UK companies that are first placers of forest-risk commodities on the EU market, the cost reduction will be considerably smaller."

For large and medium-sized primary operators — the category most UK importers and traders fall into — the obligations remain substantially intact. The three-step due diligence process of information gathering, risk assessment, and risk mitigation continues to apply. Geolocation of every plot is still required. The DDS still has to be filed in the EU information system before products are placed on the market.

What changes for UK operators in practice

Three areas of practical change deserve attention.

Product scope adjustments

Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/2102, in force since 18 September 2026, amended Annex I, the list of in-scope CN codes. It removed cattle hides, skins and leather and several rubber articles, and added soluble coffee and certain palm oil derivatives, which come into scope on 30 December 2027. Printed products under Chapter 49 of the Combined Nomenclature had already left scope in December 2025. Wood, paper and furniture entries are unchanged. UK operators handling palm and coffee derivatives should rerun their product scope analysis against the amended annex.

Treatment of upstream evidence and certification

The updated guidance is expected to clarify how operators can rely on upstream DDS identifiers and on third-party certification evidence within their risk assessment. This is welcome, but it should not be misread. Certification under FSC, PEFC, ISCC or comparable schemes does not replace EUDR due diligence. It can support a risk assessment, but the operator remains responsible for the conclusion. Companies that have built EUDR strategies around heavy reliance on certificates need to test that assumption against the new guidance carefully.

Information system functionality

The Commission has confirmed revisions to the implementing regulation governing the EU information system, addressing technical issues that surfaced during the limited operational window earlier in 2026. UK operators that have already begun trialling DDS submissions should expect interface changes and updated data validation rules in the run-up to December 2026.

The UK angle — what the simplification does not change

The simplification package does not touch UK law, and UK law has since moved. On 23 June 2026 Defra confirmed that Great Britain will have its own due diligence rules for wood, cattle, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber and soy, applying to businesses with annual turnover above £1 million. They will be made under the Environment Act 2021 alongside a strengthened UK Timber Regulation (UKTR), with legislation expected in 2027 after consultation.

Until then UKTR continues to apply. UK importers placing timber and timber products on the GB market remain subject to UKTR due diligence, separately from any EUDR obligation that arises from placing the same products on the EU market. The GB rules will test legal production, where EUDR also tests deforestation after 31 December 2020, so the two standards will differ even once the new rules are in force.

The data requirements are converging. Defra's stated aim is that a GB business will hold broadly the same information, including geolocation, as an EUDR due diligence statement requires. A UK company supplying both markets should build one evidence file to the EUDR standard and expect to use it for both. EUDR itself applies in Northern Ireland from 30 December 2026. Practical priorities for the next six months

With application from 30 December 2026 confirmed, UK operators have under three months to finalise their systems. The simplification package does not justify slowing that work down. Practical priorities:

  • Re-confirm the size classification of your business under the EUDR's micro, small, medium and large categories — this drives almost every obligation
  • Identify which entities in your supply chain are first placers and which are downstream, since the simplified obligations depend on it
  • Review your product scope against Annex I as amended by Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/2102, particularly for palm and coffee derivatives
  • Test geolocation data quality now — plot-level coordinates remain mandatory for every primary operator above the SME threshold
  • Update due diligence procedures and supplier onboarding documentation against the consolidated guidance once published
  • Plan EU information system testing and DDS submission rehearsals well before December 2026

What this means in practice

The April 2026 simplification package is genuine, but it is not a reset. It clarifies the implementation route and reduces friction at the SME and downstream end of the supply chain, while leaving the core obligations on first placers fully intact. UK operators should treat it as a useful refinement of a regulation they need to comply with — not as an invitation to slow down preparation. The deadlines have not moved, the geolocation and legality requirements have not changed, and the EU competent authorities will be looking to demonstrate enforcement credibility from day one.
