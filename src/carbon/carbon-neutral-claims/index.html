Carbon Neutral Claims: What Verification Has to Show

Since 27 September 2026 a product sold to EU consumers cannot be called carbon neutral on the strength of offsets. UK law has no equivalent ban. The CMA and the ASA ask what the claim rests on, and the answer has to be on file.

Key points

  • EU: offset-based climate claims on products have been prohibited since 27 September 2026. Claims about future performance need a published plan and independent monitoring
  • UK: no ban, but the CMA can fine up to 10% of global turnover for a misleading claim, and a retailer shares responsibility for a claim its supplier made
  • A calculated footprint is the company's own figure. Verification is an independent check of it against ISO 14064-3
  • A neutrality claim needs a verified footprint, a reduction plan and retired credits, in that order

In August 2025 a regional court in Frankfurt barred Apple from advertising a watch in Germany as its first "CO2-neutral product". The claim relied on a eucalyptus plantation in Paraguay, and leases over 75% of the project area were secured only until 2029. Deutsche Umwelthilfe brought the case under German competition law, a year before the EU's new rules applied.

What the EU now prohibits

Directive (EU) 2024/825, Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition (ECGT), has applied since 27 September 2026. It adds to the list of commercial practices that are unfair in all circumstances any claim, based on the offsetting of greenhouse gas emissions, that a product has a neutral, reduced or positive impact on the climate. "Carbon neutral", "climate neutral" and "CO2 compensated" on a pack or a product page are prohibited where purchased credits do the work.

Claims about the future are restricted as well. "Net zero by 2040" needs a public commitment, a detailed and realistic implementation plan with measurable, time-bound targets, and regular checking by an independent expert whose findings consumers can see. Generic claims such as "climate friendly" need recognised excellent environmental performance behind them.

The directive covers any business selling to EU consumers, wherever it is based, and gives no sell-through period for stock already printed. A UK exporter with "carbon neutral" on its packaging has to change the packaging. It may still say that it buys credits or funds climate projects, provided it does not present the product as neutral because of them.

Where the UK stands

UK law prohibits misleading claims without naming offsets. Since April 2025 the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 has let the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) fine a business directly, up to 10% of global turnover.

On 22 January 2026 the CMA published guidance on green claims across the supply chain. A retailer that sells a product carrying a manufacturer's claim can be liable for it alongside the manufacturer, and each business is expected to check the information it receives from suppliers before passing it on.

The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) applies the same test to advertisements. Its guidance requires the basis of a carbon neutral claim, including how far it relies on offsetting, to appear close to the claim. It upheld complaints against BrewDog for "carbon negative" and Charles Tyrwhitt for "100% carbon neutral" because the advertisements gave no basis.

Three claims, three evidence files

ClaimExampleWhat must be on file
A footprint figure"This table has a footprint of 42 kg CO2e"A product carbon footprint to ISO 14067, with the life-cycle boundary stated
A reduction"Emissions down 30% since 2020"Inventories for both years on the same boundary and method, to ISO 14064-1 or the GHG Protocol
Neutrality"A carbon neutral company"A verified footprint, a reduction plan, and records of the credits retired, to ISO 14068-1

The neutrality claim remains available in the UK at company level with its basis stated. On a product sold to EU consumers it is no longer available where it rests on offsets.

Calculation, verification and validation

A footprint that a company or its consultant has calculated is a self-declared figure. Verification is an independent body's check of that figure for a past period: whether the boundary is complete, the activity data is accurate and the method has been applied correctly. Validation is the equivalent check on a statement about the future, and tests whether the assumptions behind a target are reasonable.

ISO 14064-3 sets out how both are done, and ISO/IEC 17029 sets the requirements for the bodies that do them. A body cannot verify an inventory it helped to prepare.

ISO 14068-1, published in November 2023, replaced PAS 2060 as the standard for carbon neutrality. It puts reductions ahead of credits and requires a carbon neutrality management plan.

Where the numbers fail

For a business handling timber, paper or agricultural commodities, most emissions sit in the supply chain under Scope 3, and the least reliable part is land-use change. A footprint built on industry-average factors for timber of unknown origin can misstate the figure by a wide margin in either direction.

The plot-level origin data that the EU Deforestation Regulation requires is the same data a land-use emissions calculation needs. Our article on supply chain traceability sets out the record to hold.

What to do now

List every carbon claim in use: packaging, product pages, tender documents, the sustainability report. For each one, write down what it rests on. Remove offset-based claims from products sold into the EU, and where a company-level claim stays, state its basis beside it. Have the inventory verified before the claim is published. Ask suppliers for the evidence behind any claim printed on goods you resell.
